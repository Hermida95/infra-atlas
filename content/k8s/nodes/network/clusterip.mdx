A ClusterIP is an address from the service CIDR -- a range configured on the API server and
belonging to no network interface anywhere. Its only existence is as rules on every node. A
packet from a Pod to a ClusterIP is destination-NATed by the node's kernel to one of the
Service's endpoint Pod IPs, then routed normally by the CNI.

**Several consequences follow, and all of them come up in debugging.** You cannot ping it: ICMP
is not forwarded by those rules even when TCP works. You will never see it in a packet capture
on the receiving Pod, because by then the destination has been rewritten -- a Pod receiving
traffic through a Service sees its own IP as the destination and the caller's Pod IP as the
source. And a NetworkPolicy cannot usefully name a ClusterIP, because policy is enforced on Pod
addresses after translation; policies are written with Pod and namespace selectors for exactly
this reason.

**Load balancing is per connection and effectively random.** The default iptables mode picks an
endpoint probabilistically per new connection, with no awareness of load, latency or locality.
So a long-lived connection stays pinned to one Pod for its lifetime -- which is why a gRPC or
HTTP/2 client with connection reuse sends everything to one replica and your other replicas sit
idle, and why that case needs client-side load balancing, a headless Service, or a mesh.

**`sessionAffinity: ClientIP`** gives sticky routing by source address, with a configurable
timeout. It is a blunt instrument -- behind a NAT, every client looks like one address -- and
usually the wrong answer to a problem better solved by not keeping state in the Pod.

**The service CIDR is fixed at cluster creation** and cannot be resized on most clusters, so
running out of Service addresses is a cluster rebuild. It is also entirely separate from the Pod
CIDR, which is why both have to be planned not to overlap with anything your VPC may ever route.
