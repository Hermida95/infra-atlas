A Node is a cluster-scoped object with capacity, allocatable resources, labels, taints and a
list of conditions. The kubelet creates and maintains it -- which has an odd consequence worth
knowing: a node joins the cluster by registering itself, and deleting the Node object does not
remove the machine, it just makes the cluster forget it until its kubelet registers again.

**Two heartbeats, for different reasons.** The kubelet renews a small `Lease` object in
`kube-node-lease` every ten seconds, and updates the much larger Node status only when
something changes or every few minutes. Splitting them is what lets a cluster with thousands of
nodes heartbeat cheaply. If the lease stops being renewed, the node lifecycle controller marks
`Ready` as `Unknown` after a grace period of around forty seconds.

**What happens next is the part that confuses people during an incident.** `NotReady` does not
move Pods. The controller adds a `node.kubernetes.io/unreachable` taint with
`NoExecute`, and every Pod carries a default toleration for it with
`tolerationSeconds: 300`. So Pods stay bound to a dead node for five more minutes before they
are deleted and rescheduled. The total time from a node vanishing to replacements running is
therefore closer to six minutes than to six seconds, and the only ways to shorten it are
lowering those tolerations in the Pod spec or having a cloud controller confirm the instance is
gone, which deletes the Node and its Pods at once.

**Pressure conditions taint automatically**, which is a neat piece of design: a node under
`MemoryPressure` gets a taint that stops new BestEffort Pods landing on it, so the problem does
not compound while eviction tries to fix it. Nothing has to notice or intervene.

For capacity questions, `kubectl describe node` is the authoritative view: `Capacity` versus
`Allocatable` (the difference is reserved for the system), and underneath, the requests and
limits of every Pod on it as a percentage. That table, not `top`, is what the scheduler sees.
