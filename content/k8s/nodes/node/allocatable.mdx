`kubectl describe node` prints `Capacity` and `Allocatable`, and the gap between them is where a
surprising amount of cluster capacity goes. Capacity is what the machine has. Allocatable is
capacity minus `--kube-reserved` (for the kubelet and the container runtime), minus
`--system-reserved` (for the OS, sshd, the log agent), minus the hard eviction threshold. Only
allocatable is available to Pods.

**The numbers are not small.** On a managed cluster the reservation scales with instance size,
and on small nodes it is brutal -- a 2 vCPU, 4 GiB node can offer well under 3 GiB to Pods, so
two 1 GiB Pods plus a DaemonSet or two is already most of it. This is the main argument for
fewer, larger nodes: the fixed overhead per node is paid once per node, as are the DaemonSets,
the kube-proxy, the CNI agent and the log shipper.

**Not reserving is worse than reserving too much.** With no reservation, the scheduler happily
allocates every last byte to Pods, and then the kubelet and containerd are competing with
workloads for memory. The failure mode is a node that goes `NotReady` under load -- the kubelet
cannot get CPU to heartbeat -- which looks like a network problem and is really a budgeting one.
Enforcing the reservation with `--enforce-node-allocatable` puts the system daemons in their own
cgroup so they actually get what was set aside.

**Two further deductions catch people out.** Pod overhead from a `RuntimeClass` (meaningful for
Kata or gVisor, where each Pod carries a VM) is charged against allocatable in addition to the
containers' own requests. And a `hugepages` or GPU resource is its own dimension that does not
substitute for memory.

The practical check on any cluster is the ratio of total requests to total allocatable, per
node. Under about 60% on average means money is being burned; over about 85% means a single node
failure has nowhere to put its Pods, and the cluster autoscaler will be the only thing standing
between you and a wave of `Pending`.
