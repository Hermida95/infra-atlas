`status.phase` has five values and they are coarse on purpose; the detail is in
`status.containerStatuses`, where each container has a state (`waiting`, `running`,
`terminated`), a reason, and a `lastState` for the previous attempt. Debugging a Pod is mostly
knowing which of those fields answers your question.

**Pending splits in two and the split decides who you talk to.** If `spec.nodeName` is empty,
the scheduler could not place it, and `kubectl describe pod` ends with a line like
`0/6 nodes are available: 3 Insufficient cpu, 3 node(s) had untolerated taint` -- a capacity or
placement problem. If a node is assigned and it is still Pending, the kubelet is working:
pulling an image, attaching a volume, or waiting on something that does not exist. Those are
completely different fixes, and the field tells you which for free.

**CrashLoopBackOff is a symptom with one reliable source.** The container started and exited, so
the application has already said why -- in the logs of the attempt that died, which are gone
from `kubectl logs` and available from `kubectl logs --previous`. The second thing to check is
`lastState.terminated.exitCode`: 137 means it was OOM-killed (raise the memory limit or fix the
leak), 143 means SIGTERM, 1 or 2 means the process itself failed, and 127 means the entrypoint
is not there -- usually a wrong command or a missing shell in a distroless image.

**Running is not ready, and the distinction is the entire point of probes.** A Pod is `Ready`
only when its readiness probes pass, and only Ready Pods are in a Service's endpoints. A
deployment that rolls out "successfully" but serves 502s is almost always a readiness probe that
is too permissive -- a TCP check that passes before the app can answer, or no probe at all.

**The restart counter is cumulative and resets with the Pod.** A Pod with 400 restarts that is
currently healthy is still a problem report: something kills it periodically, and the pattern in
`kubectl get pod -w` plus the previous logs is where the answer is.
