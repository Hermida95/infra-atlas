A Job creates Pods and tracks how many have succeeded. When `completions` Pods have exited 0, the
Job is complete and stops creating more. That is the whole contract, and it makes Jobs the right
object for migrations, imports, batch processing and anything else with an end -- as opposed to a
Deployment, which will restart your migration forever.

**`restartPolicy` must be `OnFailure` or `Never`**, and the choice decides where the evidence
lives. With `OnFailure`, the kubelet restarts the container in the same Pod, so you get one Pod
with a restart count and `--previous` logs. With `Never`, a failure creates a new Pod, so you get
a trail of failed Pods you can inspect individually -- usually what you want for a batch job,
because the failed attempt is still there to read.

**`backoffLimit` and its backoff are the reason a broken Job is slow to fail.** Six retries with
exponential backoff from ten seconds means several minutes before the Job reports `Failed`, which
in a pipeline waiting on it looks like a hang. For a migration gating a deploy, a lower limit and
an `activeDeadlineSeconds` are what turn a hang into a clear failure.

**Indexed completion mode is the underused feature.** With
`completionMode: Indexed`, each Pod gets a distinct `JOB_COMPLETION_INDEX`, so a Job can shard a
dataset across N Pods with no coordination and no queue -- the simplest parallel batch primitive
Kubernetes has. Combined with `parallelism` it gives you a worker pool with a known total.

**Cleanup is opt-in and that is the most common operational complaint.** Without
`ttlSecondsAfterFinished`, every Job and every Pod it created stays in the API forever. A CronJob
running every five minutes leaves thousands of objects behind, which inflates etcd, slows down
`kubectl get pods`, and eventually shows up as control plane latency. Setting a TTL of an hour or
a day, plus `successfulJobsHistoryLimit` on the CronJob, is the fix nobody regrets.
