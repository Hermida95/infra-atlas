A ReplicaSet does one thing: count the Pods matching its selector and create or delete Pods until
the count equals `spec.replicas`. That is the entire controller. It has no concept of versions,
updates or ordering -- which is exactly why Deployments exist on top of it.

**You read them, you do not write them.** During a rolling update, `kubectl get rs` shows the old
ReplicaSet going 3 → 2 → 1 → 0 and the new one 0 → 1 → 2 → 3, each with
DESIRED, CURRENT and READY columns. That table is the clearest view of a rollout there is: a new
ReplicaSet whose CURRENT climbs but whose READY stays at zero means the new Pods are starting and
failing their readiness probe, and the rollout will stall there until the progress deadline
fires.

**Adoption is the surprising behaviour.** A ReplicaSet will adopt any Pod that matches its
selector and has no controller owner -- so a bare Pod you created with the same labels can be
taken over, counted, and deleted when the ReplicaSet decides it has too many. This is also why
two Deployments with overlapping selectors fight: each sees the other's Pods as its own and
deletes them to get back to its count.

**Scaling down is not random.** The controller prefers to delete Pods that are unscheduled, then
Pods in a worse phase, then Pods on nodes with more replicas of the same set, then the youngest.
In practice that means a scale-down leans towards leaving you a balanced, healthy, longer-running
set -- but it is a heuristic, not a guarantee, and nothing stops it from taking the Pod that was
mid-request. That is what `terminationGracePeriodSeconds` and a `preStop` hook are for.

`kubectl describe rs` is worth knowing for one line: `ReplicaFailure`, with the actual API error
the controller got when it tried to create a Pod. A Deployment that produces no Pods at all --
not Pending, not failing, simply absent -- is nearly always a quota denial or an admission
webhook rejection recorded exactly there, and nowhere else.
