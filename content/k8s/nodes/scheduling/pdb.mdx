A PodDisruptionBudget says how much of a workload may be unavailable at once *because someone
asked*. It is enforced by the eviction API, which is what `kubectl drain`, the cluster
autoscaler, and most node upgrade tooling use -- so it is the mechanism that stops a cluster
upgrade from rolling through your three replicas in thirty seconds.

**Voluntary only, and the distinction is absolute.** A PDB has no effect on a Pod that crashes,
a node that dies, a kernel OOM kill, or node-pressure eviction. It is not a guarantee of
availability; it is a constraint on deliberate, tool-driven disruption. Reading it as the former
is how people end up believing a budget protects them from a zone failure.

**`maxUnavailable` is usually the right field.** `minAvailable: 2` on a Deployment that later
scales to 10 still only protects 2, and on one that scales down to 2 blocks all disruption
forever. `maxUnavailable: 1` keeps meaning the same thing at any size. And
`minAvailable` equal to the replica count is the classic self-inflicted deadlock: no eviction is
ever permitted, so no node holding one of those Pods can ever be drained, so cluster upgrades and
scale-down stop.

**The stuck-drain diagnosis is almost always a budget.** `kubectl drain` hanging with
`Cannot evict pod as it would violate the disruption budget` means either the budget is too
tight, or -- very commonly -- the workload is already below it because some of its Pods are
unhealthy, so no further eviction is allowed even though those Pods are useless.
`unhealthyPodEvictionPolicy: AlwaysAllow` is the field that was added for exactly this, and it
belongs on most budgets.

**Single-replica workloads should not have one.** A PDB over one Pod with
`maxUnavailable: 0` makes the node undrainable; with `maxUnavailable: 1` it permits everything
and the budget is decoration. If the workload matters, give it two replicas and then a budget; if
it does not, leave the budget off so cluster maintenance can proceed.
