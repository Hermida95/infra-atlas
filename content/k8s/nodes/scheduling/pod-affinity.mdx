Pod affinity places a Pod relative to other Pods: near ones matching a label selector, or away
from them. The `topologyKey` decides what "near" and "away" mean -- `kubernetes.io/hostname` for
per-node, `topology.kubernetes.io/zone` for per-zone -- and without it the rule is meaningless,
which is why the field is mandatory.

**Anti-affinity is the half everyone uses.** "No two replicas of this Deployment on the same
node" is the classic high-availability rule, written as required anti-affinity on
`app: myapp` with `topologyKey: kubernetes.io/hostname`. It works, and it has a sharp edge: with
three replicas and two nodes, the third Pod is `Pending` forever, because required means
required. On a cluster that scales nodes, that is fine. On a fixed three-node cluster during a
node upgrade, it is an outage you built deliberately.

**Which is why topology spread constraints largely replaced it.** `maxSkew` expresses "spread
these evenly, with a tolerance" rather than "never together", and
`whenUnsatisfiable: ScheduleAnyway` degrades instead of blocking. For the common goal --
replicas across zones and nodes, without pinning yourself into a corner -- spread is the better
tool and the scheduler evaluates it far more cheaply.

**The remaining good uses of required anti-affinity** are the cases where co-location is actually
unacceptable: etcd or ZooKeeper members that must not share a failure domain, two Pods competing
for the same local disk or the same host port. For everything else, `preferred` anti-affinity
with a high weight gets you spread without the deadlock.

**Affinity (the positive form) is genuinely rare**, and worth questioning when you see it. The
legitimate case is latency: a sidecar-less cache or a sharded store where the reader must be on
the same node as its shard. The illegitimate case is trying to recreate a Pod -- if two things
must always be together, they belong in one Pod.

Watch the performance note in large clusters: required pod affinity rules are evaluated against
every Pod in each candidate topology domain, and a cluster with tens of thousands of Pods and
widespread required rules measurably slows scheduling for everyone.
