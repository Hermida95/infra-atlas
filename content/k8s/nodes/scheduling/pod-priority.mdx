A `PriorityClass` is a cluster-scoped object holding a name and an integer. A Pod referencing one
via `priorityClassName` gets that value in `spec.priority`, and two things follow: the scheduler
orders its queue by priority, so higher-priority pending Pods are considered first; and if a
high-priority Pod cannot be placed anywhere, the scheduler may evict lower-priority Pods to make
space for it.

**Preemption is the part that surprises people.** It is not an administrative tool -- it happens
automatically. The scheduler picks a node where evicting some set of lower-priority Pods would
let the pending Pod fit, deletes them with their grace periods, and schedules. The evicted Pods
go back to `Pending` and are rescheduled if there is room, which there may not be. So setting a
high priority on a workload is a statement that other people's workloads may be killed for it.

**It respects PodDisruptionBudgets on a best-effort basis only.** The scheduler prefers victims
whose budgets would not be violated, but if no such set exists it will violate a budget rather
than leave the high-priority Pod pending. This is documented, deliberate, and worth knowing
before you rely on a PDB as a guarantee.

**The two built-in classes are for the cluster, not for you.**
`system-cluster-critical` and `system-node-critical` sit above everything and are meant for the
control plane and node agents -- CoreDNS, kube-proxy, the CNI. Using them for an application
means your app can preempt the networking its own Pods depend on, which fails in an entertaining
way.

**The best trick it enables is warm capacity.** Create a very low (even negative) priority class,
run a Deployment of Pods that do nothing but request resources, and size it to one node's worth.
The cluster looks full, the autoscaler keeps that extra node, and the instant a real Pod needs
room the placeholders are preempted -- giving you a node already running rather than a
three-minute wait for an instance to boot. It is the standard answer to "the autoscaler is too
slow for our traffic spikes".

A sane scheme is three or four classes with wide gaps: batch below default, default, and
latency-critical above. Dozens of finely graded priorities produce preemption cascades nobody can
reason about.
