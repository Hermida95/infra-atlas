A label is a key-value pair in metadata that anything can query. That is the entire mechanism
by which Kubernetes objects relate to one another, and it explains a property people find
surprising: almost nothing in the system refers to another object by name. A Service does not
list its Pods. A Deployment does not list its Pods. They both hold a query, and the answer
changes as Pods come and go.

**This is why rolling updates work at all.** New Pods appear carrying the same `app` label, the
Service's query starts matching them, the EndpointSlice is rewritten by a controller, and
traffic shifts -- with nobody told and nothing reconfigured. It is also why a typo in a label
produces no error at all: a selector that matches nothing is perfectly valid and simply selects
nothing, so the Service exists, has no endpoints, and returns connection refused.

**A selector is a contract, so it cannot drift.** `spec.selector` on a Deployment is immutable
after creation, precisely because changing it would orphan the Pods the old query matched. When
you need a different selector, you create a new Deployment and delete the old one. The one on a
Service is mutable, which makes a Service a useful switch: point it at a different `version`
label and traffic moves, with no change to either Deployment.

**Labels are for selection, annotations are for everything else.** Labels are indexed and
queryable and therefore size-limited and syntax-constrained; if nothing will ever select on a
value, it does not belong in a label. The standard `app.kubernetes.io/*` set is worth adopting
even in a small cluster, because Helm, Kustomize, dashboards and most operators assume it, and
`kubectl get pods -l app.kubernetes.io/part-of=checkout` is the difference between debugging one
application and debugging a cluster.

The debugging habit that pays off most: when a Service has no endpoints, compare
`kubectl get svc <name> -o yaml` against `kubectl get pods --show-labels`. Nine times in ten the
selector and the labels differ by one character.
