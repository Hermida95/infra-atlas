A namespace is a name scope. Everything else people believe about it -- that it isolates, that
it contains, that it protects -- is true only because something else was also configured, and
assuming otherwise is how a staging workload ends up reading production traffic.

**What it does on its own:** names are unique within it, DNS names include it, RBAC rules bind
to it, and ResourceQuota and LimitRange live in it. That is a genuinely useful bundle, and it is
why the namespace, not the cluster, is the normal unit to hand to a team.

**What it does not do:** it does not stop traffic. All Pods in a cluster can reach all other
Pods across namespaces unless a NetworkPolicy says otherwise -- and most clusters have none.
It does not stop a Pod from mounting a hostPath, from running as root, or from reading a node's
credentials, unless Pod Security Admission or a policy engine is enforcing a level. And it does
nothing at all for cluster-scoped objects: a ClusterRole, a PersistentVolume, a CRD and a Node
are shared by everyone, whatever namespace they were applied from.

**The tenancy question that follows** is therefore not "namespace or cluster" but "what does
this tenant have to be prevented from doing". A namespace plus quota plus a default-deny
NetworkPolicy plus a restricted Pod Security level plus no CRD or ClusterRole access is a
reasonable soft boundary for teams inside one company. It is not a boundary for untrusted code,
because the kernel is shared and so is the control plane; that needs separate clusters or a
sandboxed runtime.

Two operational details worth knowing. A namespace stuck in `Terminating` is almost always an
object with a finalizer nobody will clear, often a custom resource whose operator has been
uninstalled. And `kubectl delete namespace` is the fastest destructive command in Kubernetes:
it removes everything inside, including PersistentVolumeClaims and therefore, depending on the
reclaim policy, the data.
