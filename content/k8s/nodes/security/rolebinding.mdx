A binding joins subjects to a role. A RoleBinding lives in a namespace and grants within it; a
ClusterRoleBinding is cluster-scoped and grants everywhere. The crucial detail is that **the
binding's kind decides the scope, not the role's**: a RoleBinding referencing a ClusterRole grants
those permissions only inside the binding's namespace.

**That combination is the main tool for multi-tenancy.** Define the permission set once as a
ClusterRole -- or use the built-in `edit` -- and create a RoleBinding per team namespace. No
duplicated rules, no cluster-wide grant, and adding a namespace is one small object.

**Subjects come in three kinds and the syntax differs.** A `ServiceAccount` subject needs
`name` and `namespace`. A `User` or `Group` subject is a bare string, matched against whatever
authentication returned -- so `kind: User, name: alice@example.com` only works if your OIDC
provider puts exactly that in the username claim. Binding to a group is almost always the right
choice for humans: membership is then managed in the identity provider, and nobody has to edit
RBAC when someone joins or leaves.

**Bindings are nearly immutable in one respect:** `roleRef` cannot be changed after creation. To
point a binding at a different role you delete and recreate it, which is a brief window with no
access -- worth knowing before doing it to something in the critical path.

**ClusterRoleBindings are the audit target.** `kubectl get clusterrolebindings -o wide` lists
every cluster-wide grant with its subjects, and the ones to question are any binding of
`cluster-admin` to a ServiceAccount, anything granting to the group
`system:authenticated` or `system:unauthenticated`, and bindings to a ServiceAccount in a
namespace where many people can create Pods -- because creating a Pod with that ServiceAccount
inherits the grant. That last path is how a seemingly modest namespace permission becomes cluster
admin.
