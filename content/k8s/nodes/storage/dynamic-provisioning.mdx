Dynamic provisioning is the loop that makes a PersistentVolumeClaim produce storage: the
external-provisioner sidecar sees a claim naming a StorageClass, calls `CreateVolume` on the CSI
driver with the class's parameters, and creates a matching PersistentVolume bound to the claim.
No administrator is involved, which is the entire point.

**It turns storage into self-service**, and that is both the benefit and the risk. A developer
asking for 100Gi gets 100Gi, billed monthly, with no approval step. The controls that make this
sustainable are a ResourceQuota limiting `requests.storage` (optionally per class, so the
expensive class is capped tighter), and classes that encode sensible defaults rather than
offering the most expensive disk by accident.

**`WaitForFirstConsumer` is the setting that makes it correct in a multi-zone cluster.** With
immediate binding, the volume is created before anything knows where the Pod will run, so the
Pod becomes pinned to whatever zone the provisioner picked -- and if that zone is full, the Pod
is unschedulable with a volume already paid for. Deferring the creation until the scheduler has
chosen inverts the dependency properly.

**Deleting is where the money goes.** With `reclaimPolicy: Delete`, removing the claim removes
the disk -- clean, and dangerous for data. With `Retain`, the disk survives as a
`Released` volume that no new claim will bind to, which is safe and leaves orphans that have to
be found and deleted by hand. Teams that pick `Retain` for safety and never do the second part
accumulate a long tail of cost, and an inventory check of unbound volumes is worth scheduling.

**The remaining case for static provisioning** is storage that already exists: a volume restored
from a snapshot, an NFS export managed elsewhere, a disk with data you cannot afford to have
recreated. Then you write the PersistentVolume by hand and a claim with a matching
`volumeName`, and dynamic provisioning stays out of it.
