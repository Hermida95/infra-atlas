A PersistentVolumeClaim is what a workload actually writes: "I need 20Gi, ReadWriteOnce, from this
StorageClass". The control plane either binds it to an existing PersistentVolume or asks the
driver to create one. The Pod then references the claim, not the volume, which is the indirection
that lets storage outlive Pods.

**It is namespaced, and that is the tenancy boundary.** A Pod can only use a claim in its own
namespace, so storage follows the same ownership as everything else, and a ResourceQuota can cap
`requests.storage` per class to stop a team provisioning a terabyte by accident.

**`volumeBindingMode` is the field that prevents the classic zone deadlock.** With
`Immediate`, the volume is created as soon as the claim exists -- in some zone the provisioner
picks -- and the Pod can then only be scheduled in that zone, which fails if there is no room
there. With `WaitForFirstConsumer`, binding is deferred until a Pod using the claim is being
scheduled, so the volume is created in the zone the scheduler chose. On any multi-zone cluster
the second is what you want, and it is the default on most managed clusters now.

**Deletion is deliberately awkward**, for good reasons. A claim in use carries the
`kubernetes.io/pvc-protection` finalizer, so `kubectl delete pvc` hangs in `Terminating` until
no Pod mounts it -- which is a feature, not a bug. And a StatefulSet's claims are not deleted
when the StatefulSet is, so scaling from 5 to 3 leaves two claims (and two billed volumes)
waiting for a scale-up. `persistentVolumeClaimRetentionPolicy` now lets you choose that
behaviour explicitly, and setting it on purpose is better than inheriting it.

**A Pending claim has three causes and `kubectl describe pvc` names which.** No StorageClass can
satisfy it (wrong class name, or no default class in the cluster); the requested access mode is
not available from the driver; or, with `WaitForFirstConsumer`, nothing is wrong at all and it is
simply waiting for a Pod -- which is the one that looks like a failure and is not.
