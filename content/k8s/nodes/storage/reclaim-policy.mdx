A PersistentVolume's `persistentVolumeReclaimPolicy` decides what happens when its claim is
deleted. `Delete` removes the volume object and asks the driver to destroy the real disk.
`Retain` keeps both, marking the volume `Released`. Dynamically provisioned volumes inherit the
policy from their StorageClass, and that inheritance is where the decision is actually made.

**`Delete` is the default and it means `kubectl delete namespace` destroys data.** That single
command removes the claims, which removes the volumes, which removes the disks -- no
confirmation, no recycle bin, and nothing to restore from unless you took snapshots. For a
development namespace that is a convenience. For anything with a database in it, it is the most
dangerous command available to a cluster user, and it is why a `Retain` class for stateful
workloads is worth the extra work.

**`Retain` is safe and leaves a job.** A released volume will not be reused: it still carries the
`claimRef` of the claim that is gone, so a new identical claim will not bind to it. Reusing it
means clearing that reference by hand (`kubectl patch pv ... --type=merge -p
'{"spec":{"claimRef":null}}'`) so it becomes `Available` again. Deleting it means deleting the PV
object *and* then the real disk in the cloud, because removing the object with
`Retain` leaves the disk behind, billing silently.

**That orphan accumulation is a real cost line.** Clusters that use `Retain` without a cleanup
habit end up with dozens of unattached disks nobody can map back to a workload. A scheduled
report of PersistentVolumes in `Released` state, and of cloud disks with no attachment, is the
cheap counter-measure.

**Neither policy is a backup.** A reclaim policy decides whether deletion propagates; it does
nothing about corruption, a bad migration, or ransomware. VolumeSnapshots or a backup tool like
Velero with a snapshot provider are what give you a version to go back to, and the reclaim policy
only decides how easy it is to lose the current one.
