The HorizontalPodAutoscaler reads a metric, compares it with a target, and writes a new replica
count. The arithmetic is simple: desired replicas equals current replicas times (current metric
divided by target metric), rounded up, clamped to min and max. Everything surprising about it
comes from where the metric comes from.

**CPU utilisation is relative to the request, and this is the detail that matters most.** A target
of 70% means 70% of what the Pod *requested*, not of the node and not of the limit. So a Pod
requesting 100m and using 200m reports 200% utilisation and triggers aggressive scale-up, while
the same Pod requesting 2 cores reports 10% and never scales at all. Requests that do not
reflect reality make the HPA behave randomly, which is why fixing requests comes before tuning
autoscaling.

**`behavior` is the fix for flapping and for slow reaction.** The defaults are deliberately
cautious on the way down -- a five-minute stabilisation window, so a drop in load does not
immediately remove Pods that will be needed again -- and permissive on the way up. A workload
with a slow start benefits from a scale-up stabilisation window and a percentage policy; a
workload that must absorb a spike wants no up-stabilisation and a large step. Both are
expressible, and neither is the default.

**Two or more metrics are evaluated independently and the largest wins**, which is usually what
you want (scale on whichever pressure is real) and occasionally a trap: a memory metric that
only ever rises -- a cache, a JVM heap -- will pin the replica count at the maximum, because
memory usage does not fall when load does. Memory is a poor HPA signal for most applications.

**When it does nothing, the reason is in the conditions.** `kubectl describe hpa` shows
`ScalingActive: False` with `FailedGetResourceMetric` when metrics-server is missing or the Pods
have no requests, and `ScalingLimited: True` when it is already at `maxReplicas`. Those two lines
answer almost every "why is my HPA not working" question.
