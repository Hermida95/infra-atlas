Karpenter watches for unschedulable Pods, looks at what they actually need -- CPU, memory,
architecture, zone, taints they tolerate, volumes they use -- and launches an instance that fits,
chosen from a wide range of types. No node groups, no pre-declared shapes. When Pods go away it
removes or replaces nodes to reduce waste.

**The conceptual shift is that node shape becomes an output.** With the Cluster Autoscaler you
decide the instance types in advance and the autoscaler counts them; with Karpenter you declare
constraints -- these families, these architectures, these zones, spot or on-demand -- and the
shape is derived from the workload. A batch of Pods needing 30 cores becomes one large instance
instead of eight small ones, with less per-node overhead and fewer DaemonSet copies.

**Consolidation is the feature that saves the money and causes the surprises.** Karpenter
continuously looks for nodes it could remove by moving their Pods elsewhere, and will replace a
node with a cheaper one that still fits. That means nodes come and go during normal operation --
not only when load changes. Workloads therefore need what they should have had anyway:
PodDisruptionBudgets, correct `terminationGracePeriodSeconds`, and no state on local disk.
`do-not-disrupt` annotations exist for the genuine exceptions.

**It makes spot capacity practical.** Karpenter understands interruption notices, drains the node
before the instance goes, and can be told to prefer spot with an on-demand fallback -- so a
fault-tolerant workload runs at a fraction of the price with the handling built in rather than
scripted.

**Two things to set deliberately.** `expireAfter` on a NodePool forces nodes to be replaced
periodically, which is how you get patched node images without a migration project -- genuinely
valuable, and it means the churn is permanent. And the NodePool's instance-type requirements want
a real floor: left wide open, Karpenter will happily pick a tiny instance that technically fits
and leaves no room for the DaemonSets.
