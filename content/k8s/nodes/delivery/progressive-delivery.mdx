A rolling update's only question is whether new Pods become Ready. A version that starts
successfully and returns 500s to a third of requests passes that test and rolls through the whole
fleet. Progressive delivery adds the missing question -- is this release actually good -- and
answers it from metrics, automatically, before the release is complete.

**Argo Rollouts and Flagger are the two implementations.** Argo Rollouts replaces the Deployment
with a `Rollout` object carrying a strategy: steps of traffic weight, pauses, and
`AnalysisTemplate` references that query a metrics provider between steps. Flagger keeps the
Deployment and drives the traffic shifting around it. Both support canary and blue-green, both
integrate with meshes and ingress controllers for the traffic splitting, and both can abort and
roll back on their own.

**Traffic splitting is the prerequisite people underestimate.** Shifting 5% of traffic needs
something that can weight routes -- a mesh, an ingress controller that supports canary
annotations, or Gateway API's weighted backends. Without one, the only lever is replica counts:
1 new Pod out of 20 is roughly 5%, which is approximate, coupled to capacity, and useless below
that granularity.

**The analysis is the part that requires thought.** A canary judged on "no 500s in 60 seconds" at
5% traffic may see thirty requests -- not enough signal to conclude anything. Useful analysis
needs enough traffic and enough time per step, metrics that reflect the user (error rate and
latency at the right percentile, perhaps a conversion metric), and a baseline to compare against
rather than an absolute threshold.

**It is worth the complexity for some services and not for others.** A payment path or a
public API, where a bad release is expensive and a rollback is slow, justifies it. An internal
tool with ten users does not -- there, a rolling update with a good readiness probe and a known
`kubectl rollout undo` is the proportionate answer, and pretending otherwise just adds machinery
nobody maintains.
