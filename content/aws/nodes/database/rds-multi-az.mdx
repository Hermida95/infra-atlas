Multi-AZ is the availability feature, and the single most common mistake in RDS design is
expecting it to help with read load. In the classic **Multi-AZ instance** deployment the standby
serves nothing: no reads, no connections, nothing. It exists to take over. Replication is
synchronous, so there is no data loss, and failover takes roughly 60 to 120 seconds, during
which the endpoint's DNS record is repointed at the standby.

Because failover is DNS, the application has to cooperate. A connection pool that caches the
resolved address, or a JVM with an infinite DNS cache, will keep trying the dead instance long
after AWS has finished failing over. Short TTLs and a pool that retries on connection failure
are part of the design, not an afterthought.

The newer **Multi-AZ DB cluster** deployment changes the trade-off: one writer and **two
readable standbys** across three zones, semi-synchronous replication, failover typically under
35 seconds, and a reader endpoint that actually serves traffic. It is available for MySQL and
PostgreSQL and costs more, but it collapses the "Multi-AZ or read replica" question into one
answer where it is supported.

What triggers a failover is broader than a disaster: a zone outage, an instance or storage
failure, and also **planned maintenance** -- patching and instance resizing are applied to the
standby and then failed over, which turns an hour of downtime into about a minute. That is a
real operational benefit on its own.

The exam distinction to keep crisp: **Multi-AZ is availability, within one region, synchronous,
automatic.** **Read replicas are read scale, asynchronous, possibly cross-region, promoted
manually.** They are not alternatives, and a scenario that states both requirements wants both
features.
