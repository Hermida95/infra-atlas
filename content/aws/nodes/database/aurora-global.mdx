An Aurora Global Database is one cluster spanning regions: a primary that takes writes and up to
five secondaries that serve reads. Replication happens at the **storage layer** over AWS's own
network, which is why the typical lag is **under a second** across continents and why it adds
almost no load to the writer -- there is no logical replication to apply.

It answers two different requirements, and it is worth being clear which one is in play.
**Latency**: readers in Singapore and São Paulo serve local traffic at local speed, with writes
still going to one region. **Disaster recovery**: an RPO measured in seconds and an RTO of about
a minute, which is what makes a warm-standby design meet objectives that backup-and- restore
cannot approach.

Two failover modes. **Managed planned failover** switches the primary cleanly with no data loss,
for a rehearsal or a region migration. **Unplanned promotion** takes a secondary in an emergency
and may lose the sub-second tail of writes. There is also **write forwarding**, where a
secondary accepts writes and forwards them to the primary -- convenient for an application that
cannot route writes itself, at the cost of the round trip.

What it does not do is move your traffic. Promoting the database is half a failover; something
still has to send users to the new region, and that is Route 53 health checks and failover
records, or Global Accelerator. A DR plan with a global database and no traffic-steering
mechanism is a plan that fails over the data and leaves the users looking at the dead region.
