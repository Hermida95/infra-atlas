Global Accelerator gives you two **static anycast IP addresses** advertised from AWS edge
locations worldwide. A client connects to the nearest edge, and the traffic crosses the rest of
the distance on AWS's own backbone rather than the public internet.

Two benefits, and they are independent. **Performance**: the backbone is less congested and
better routed than the internet, so latency and jitter improve, typically most for users far
from the region. **Failover without DNS**: the client's IP address never changes, so moving
traffic between regions is a change inside Global Accelerator rather than a DNS record that
resolvers cache. If a requirement says failover must not depend on DNS TTLs, this is the answer
and Route 53 is not.

The controls are **endpoint groups** per region, each with a **traffic dial** (what share of
traffic that region may take) and **endpoint weights** inside it. Health checks remove an
unhealthy endpoint automatically. That is enough for blue/green across regions, gradual
migration, and shedding load from a struggling region, all without touching DNS.

Against CloudFront, which also uses the edge network: CloudFront **caches**, speaks HTTP and
runs functions at the edge. Global Accelerator caches nothing and works for **TCP and UDP** --
game servers, IoT over MQTT, VoIP, SFTP, anything non-HTTP, and any case where static IPs are
required. For a cacheable HTTP workload, CloudFront does more and usually costs less. For
non-HTTP or for static addressing, Global Accelerator is the only one of the two that applies.
It also charges a fixed hourly fee per accelerator plus data transfer, so it is not something to
leave running by accident.
