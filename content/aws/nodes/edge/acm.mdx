ACM issues and renews public TLS certificates for free, and the private key never leaves AWS --
you cannot export it, which is the point and also the limitation.

**Validation** is either DNS or email, and DNS is the one to use: a CNAME in the zone, and
renewal is then automatic and silent for as long as that record exists. With Route 53 the
console writes the record for you. Email validation needs a human every time and is how
certificates expire.

Two constraints to carry into any design. Certificates are **regional**, and they are attached
to regional services -- an ALB in eu-west-1 needs a certificate in eu-west-1. The exception that
catches everyone: **CloudFront reads certificates only from us-east-1**, because its control
plane lives there. A multi-region deployment therefore needs the same domain's certificate
issued separately in each region plus one in us-east-1 for the distribution. And ACM public
certificates **cannot be exported**, so anything that needs the key on a server you control --
an EC2 instance terminating TLS itself, an on-premises appliance -- cannot use one.

**ACM Private CA** is the other half of the service: your own certificate authority for internal
names, mutual TLS between services, device identities and IoT. It is charged per CA per month
plus per certificate, and unlike public ACM the certificates can be exported.

The detail worth knowing for an incident: renewal is automatic only while the validation record
is still in place and the certificate is still **in use** by a supported service. An unused
certificate stops renewing, and so does one whose DNS record someone cleaned up.
