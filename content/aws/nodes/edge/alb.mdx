An ALB terminates the connection, reads the HTTP request, and decides what to do with it. That
is the whole difference from an NLB, and everything in its feature list follows: routing on host
and path, header-based routing, redirects, fixed responses, sticky sessions by cookie, HTTP/2
and gRPC, WebSockets, and request tracing.

**Rules** are the routing table. A listener holds rules in priority order, each with conditions
(host, path, header, query, source IP, method) and an action. One ALB in front of several
services, routing `/api/*` to one target group and `/` to another, is the standard shape and it
is why path-based routing is the first thing people reach for when consolidating load balancers.

**Target groups** decide what is behind it, and the protocol version and health check live there
rather than on the listener. Targets can be instances, raw **IP addresses** (which is how
Fargate tasks and on-premises servers are registered), a **Lambda function**, or another ALB
behind an NLB. Two settings in a target group do most of the work in production: the **health
check** path and thresholds, and the **deregistration delay**, which is how long in-flight
requests get to finish before a target is removed -- set to zero, every deployment drops
requests.

Three features worth knowing because they remove code. **Built-in authentication**: an
`authenticate-oidc` or `authenticate-cognito` action means the load balancer handles the whole
sign-in redirect and passes verified claims to your application. **WAF** attaches directly, so
an ALB with no CloudFront in front still gets layer-7 filtering. And **weighted target groups**
on one rule give you blue/green and canary at the load balancer, with a shift that is one API
call to roll back.

Operationally: it is multi-zone by definition (at least two subnets) with **cross-zone load
balancing on and free**, unlike NLB where it is charged. It scales on its own, which means its
IP addresses change -- always use the DNS name, and an **alias** record at the zone apex. For
client IP, your application reads **`X-Forwarded-For`**, because the connection it sees comes
from the load balancer. And access logs go to S3, off by default, which is the thing you wish
you had turned on during the incident.
