EMR runs the open-source big data stack -- Spark, Hive, Presto/Trino, HBase, Flink -- on
clusters AWS provisions and configures. You get the frameworks and their tuning knobs, and you
own the cluster.

The cost model is the main reason to choose it over Glue, and it rests on **node groups**.
**Primary** nodes run the coordinators. **Core** nodes hold HDFS and should be on-demand,
because losing one loses data. **Task** nodes only compute and should be on **Spot**, often at
70-90% off, because losing one just reruns a task. That split is most of the saving, and it is
why EMR appears in cost-optimisation questions.

The architectural habit that matters is **EMRFS**: read and write S3 directly rather than
keeping data in HDFS. The cluster then becomes disposable -- spin it up for a job, tear it down
after -- and the data outlives it. **Transient clusters** for scheduled work and **long-running
clusters** for interactive use are the two shapes, and auto-termination on idle is what stops
the second becoming an expensive mistake.

Three deployment options: **EMR on EC2** (the classic, most control), **EMR Serverless** (no
cluster at all, pay for what the job uses -- the simplest place to start now), and **EMR on
EKS**, which runs Spark on an existing Kubernetes cluster so the data team shares capacity with
everyone else.

Against Glue: Glue is serverless and opinionated, good for straightforward ETL with little
operational work. EMR is for jobs that are large, long-running, need specific framework versions
or tuning, or where Spot economics on task nodes make a real difference to the bill.
