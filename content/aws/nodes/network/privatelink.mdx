PrivateLink exposes a **service**, not a network. That is the whole idea, and it is what makes
it different from peering and from a Transit Gateway, both of which join routing domains
together.

The provider puts a **Network Load Balancer** in front of whatever they are offering and creates
an **endpoint service** from it. A consumer creates an interface endpoint in their own VPC and
gets an ENI with a private address. Traffic flows one way -- consumer to provider -- over the
AWS backbone. Nothing is routed: the two VPCs have no route to each other, no knowledge of each
other's addresses, and **overlapping CIDRs stop being a problem entirely**. That last point is
the usual reason it wins. After an acquisition, two VPCs that both use 10.0.0.0/16 cannot be
peered without renumbering one of them; PrivateLink does not care.

Access is controlled by the provider through an allow-list of principals, and new requests can
require manual acceptance. Each consumer's endpoint gets its own DNS name, and the provider can
publish a private hosted zone so consumers use a friendly name instead.

Where it shows up: every AWS interface endpoint is PrivateLink underneath; SaaS vendors offer it
so customers never traverse the internet; and inside a large organization it is how a platform
team exposes one shared service -- an authentication API, an internal registry -- to a hundred
accounts without joining a hundred networks.

The trade-off against a Transit Gateway is scope. PrivateLink is one service, one direction, TCP
only, and needs an NLB. A Transit Gateway is full routing in both directions for everything. If
the requirement names a single service, PrivateLink is the smaller and safer answer.
