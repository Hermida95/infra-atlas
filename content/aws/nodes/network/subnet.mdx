A subnet is where the two decisions that shape a VPC actually get made. Choosing a subnet
chooses an Availability Zone, because a subnet lives in exactly one and never moves. And
choosing a subnet chooses reachability, because "public" and "private" are not properties of a
subnet at all -- they are a description of what its route table says. A subnet whose default
route points at an Internet Gateway is public; point the same subnet at a NAT Gateway and it is
private. Nothing else about it changes.

Five addresses in every subnet are gone before you start: the network address, the VPC router,
the DNS resolver (base + 2), one reserved for future use, and the broadcast address. A /24 is
therefore 251 usable, and a /28 -- the smallest allowed -- is 11. The largest is a /16. Size
them for what will run there, remembering that EKS gives every pod an address from the subnet by
default, which is how a cluster exhausts a /24 in an afternoon.

The usual shape is three tiers across at least two zones: public subnets holding only load
balancers and NAT gateways, private subnets with the application, and isolated subnets with no
default route at all for the databases. Six subnets for two zones, nine for three. Keep them
symmetric -- the same CIDR size in each zone -- because an Auto Scaling group that cannot
balance across zones is not actually highly available.

Two details that bite. Subnets can be **shared** with other accounts through Resource Access
Manager, which is how a platform team owns one VPC and twenty teams deploy into it without
twenty VPCs to peer. And a subnet's CIDR cannot be resized after creation: you add another
subnet instead, which is why an IPAM plan beats a spreadsheet.
