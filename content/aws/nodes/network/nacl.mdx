Network ACLs are the subnet-level firewall, and most accounts should leave the default one alone
and do their work in security groups. There are exactly two reasons to touch a NACL, and knowing
them is most of what an exam asks.

The first is **blocking**. Security groups cannot deny, so when an address has to be refused --
an abusive IP, a compromised CIDR, a partner range that must never reach a subnet -- a NACL is
the only tool below layer 7 that can do it, and it does it for everything in the subnet at once,
including resources whose security groups you do not control. The second is **defence in depth
at a boundary**: an isolated database tier where a deny-by-default NACL means a misconfigured
security group somewhere else still cannot open a path.

Stateless is the part that goes wrong. Every flow needs a rule in each direction. Allowing
inbound 443 is not enough -- the response leaves from port 443 to the client's **ephemeral
port**, so the outbound list needs 1024-65535 allowed too, and the same in reverse for traffic
your instances initiate. A NACL that permits inbound HTTPS and nothing outbound will time out
every request, and the symptom looks nothing like a firewall rule.

Rules are numbered and evaluated in order, first match wins, evaluation stops. A deny at 100 is
final even if an allow sits at 200. Number in hundreds so there is room to insert. The list ends
with an implicit `*` that denies anything unmatched; the **default** NACL that comes with a VPC
instead carries allow-all rules in both directions, which is why a brand new VPC works before
you have configured anything.
