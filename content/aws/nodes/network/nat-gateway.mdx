A NAT Gateway lets instances in private subnets start connections outward -- package
repositories, third-party APIs, AWS public endpoints -- without being reachable from outside.
AWS manages it, it scales to 100 Gbps on its own, and there is nothing to patch.

It is also one of the top two or three lines on a surprised AWS bill, because it is metered
twice: an hourly charge per gateway, and a **data processing charge per gigabyte** that is paid
on top of normal data transfer. Forty terabytes a month through a NAT Gateway costs more in
processing than most of the compute behind it. The fix is almost always the same: traffic to S3
and DynamoDB should leave through a **gateway VPC endpoint**, which has no hourly fee and no
processing charge at all, and traffic to other AWS APIs through **interface endpoints**, which
are cheaper than NAT above a modest volume. Once those are in place, what is left going through
NAT is usually small enough not to matter.

The second mistake is architectural. A NAT Gateway is **zonal**. One gateway serving all three
zones means a zone failure takes down egress for the other two, and every byte from the other
zones pays cross-zone transfer on the way. The correct shape is one gateway per zone, with each
private subnet's route table pointing at the one in its own zone. It costs more per hour and
less per gigabyte, and it survives a zone.

Worth knowing: a NAT Gateway cannot be used with a security group (it has none) and cannot
forward inbound connections. **NAT instances** -- an EC2 instance doing the same job -- still
exist and come up in exams as the cheaper, less available, you-patch-it alternative; in practice
they are a legacy answer. And IPv6 does not use NAT at all: outbound-only IPv6 is an egress-only
Internet Gateway.
