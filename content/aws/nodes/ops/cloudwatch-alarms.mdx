An alarm watches one metric and moves between three states: OK, ALARM and INSUFFICIENT_DATA.
Everything useful about alarms is in the configuration around that.

**Evaluation periods** are the first thing to get right. An alarm that fires on a single
datapoint fires on noise. "3 out of 5 datapoints" means a real, sustained condition, and the `M
out of N` form is what you want for anything that pages a human. **Treat missing data** is the
second, and it is subtle: if a service stops emitting a metric entirely -- because it is down --
an alarm set to `notBreaching` stays green. For health signals, `breaching` is usually correct.

**Anomaly detection** builds a band from the metric's own history, including its daily and
weekly shape, and alarms on departures from it. That is the right tool for anything with a
natural cycle, where a static threshold is either too loose at the peak or too tight at night.

**Composite alarms** solve alert fatigue. When a database fails, twenty service alarms fire; a
composite alarm expressing "the database alarm OR (two services AND the error rate)" pages once
and the rest stay as context. Pair it with alarm actions suppression so dependent alarms do not
notify while a parent is already in ALARM.

The actions are more than notifications. **Auto Scaling** policies are alarms underneath. **EC2
recover** restarts an instance on new hardware when the system status check fails, which handles
a class of failure with no human at all. **Systems Manager** actions can run a runbook. And
**CodeDeploy** watches alarms to roll back a deployment automatically -- which is the mechanism
that makes a canary release safe.

Two habits that matter: alarm on what users experience (latency at p99, error rate, queue depth,
request count per target) rather than on CPU, which is a symptom and arrives late; and give
every alarm a description saying what to do about it, because the person woken at 3am did not
write it.
