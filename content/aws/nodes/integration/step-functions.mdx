Step Functions moves orchestration out of your code. Instead of a Lambda that calls another
Lambda, catches its errors, sleeps and retries, you declare the states and the transitions and
the service runs them -- with the full history of every execution recorded, so debugging is
reading a timeline rather than correlating log lines.

The states that matter: **Task** does work, **Choice** branches on the data, **Parallel** runs
branches at once, **Map** fans out over an array (and **Distributed Map** does so over millions
of items from S3, at up to 10,000 concurrent child executions), and **Wait** pauses -- for a
duration or until a timestamp. That last one is quietly important: a function can no longer wait
fifteen minutes for anything, but a workflow can wait a year, and it costs nothing while it
does.

**Retry and Catch** are per state. A retry names the error types, the interval, the backoff
rate, the maximum attempts and jitter; a catch routes a failure to another state. This is the
code everyone writes badly, declared once and visible on a diagram.

**Standard versus Express** is a real decision. Standard workflows run up to a year, are
exactly-once, keep full execution history and are priced **per state transition** -- right for
long-running business processes and anything needing an audit trail. Express workflows run up to
five minutes, are at-least-once, log to CloudWatch rather than keeping history, and are priced
per request and duration -- right for high-volume event processing where per-transition pricing
would be ruinous.

Two integration patterns worth knowing by name. **`.sync`** calls a service and waits for it to
finish -- start an ECS task or a Glue job and the state does not complete until the job does,
with no polling loop. **`waitForTaskToken`** pauses the execution until something calls back
with a token, which is how a human approval, a third-party callback or a manual check becomes a
state in the workflow instead of a separate system.

Direct service integrations remove most glue functions: a workflow can write to DynamoDB,
publish to SNS, run a Batch job or call any of two hundred-odd services without a Lambda in
between.
