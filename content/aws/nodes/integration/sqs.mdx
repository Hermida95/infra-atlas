A queue decouples a producer from a consumer. The producer writes and returns; the consumer
reads when it can. The spike is absorbed by the queue rather than by whatever is behind it, and
a consumer that is down is a growing backlog rather than lost work. That is the whole value, and
it is why "the system falls over during a burst" is so often answered with a queue.

**Standard** queues have effectively unlimited throughput, deliver **at least once**, and make a
best effort at order. Both caveats are real: a message can be delivered twice, so consumers must
be **idempotent** -- usually by recording a processed id somewhere -- and order is not
guaranteed. **FIFO** queues give strict ordering within a *message group* and exactly-once
processing through a five-minute deduplication window, at 300 messages a second (3,000 with
batching, and much higher in high-throughput mode). Choose a message group id that spreads --
per customer, per order -- because ordering is only preserved within a group and a single group
is a single-threaded queue.

**Visibility timeout** is the setting that produces the strangest bugs. When a consumer receives
a message it becomes invisible for this long; if the consumer has not deleted it by then, it
reappears and someone else processes it too. A timeout shorter than the real processing time
means duplicate work that looks like a bug in the application. Set it longer than the worst
case, or call `ChangeMessageVisibility` to extend while working.

A **dead-letter queue** should exist on every queue. Without one, a message that always fails is
retried until it expires, consuming capacity and hiding the failure; with one, after
`maxReceiveCount` attempts it moves aside where it can be inspected and redriven. Alarm on its
depth.

For scaling consumers, the metric is **`ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible`** -- backlog per
worker -- not CPU. With Lambda, the event source mapping polls and scales for you; with a
container or instance fleet, target-tracking on queue depth is the textbook pattern. Enable
**long polling** everywhere (`WaitTimeSeconds: 20`): it cuts empty receives, which are both cost
and latency.
