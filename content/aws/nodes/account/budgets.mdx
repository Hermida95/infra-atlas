A budget is a threshold with an action. It can watch **cost**, **usage**, **Reserved Instance or
Savings Plans utilisation and coverage**, and it can alert on **actual** spend or on
**forecast** spend -- the second being the more useful of the two, because it fires while there
is still time to do something.

Alerts go to email or, better, to **SNS**, which means the same threshold can notify a channel
and trigger a function. **Budget actions** go further: when a threshold is crossed, apply a
restrictive IAM policy or SCP, stop EC2 instances, or stop RDS instances -- automatically or
after approval. That is the mechanism behind a sandbox account that cannot run away with the
bill.

The patterns worth setting up once: a monthly budget per account or per team tag with alerts at
80% of forecast; a budget on **untagged** spend, which makes tagging compliance visible without
a separate process; and for non-production accounts, an action that stops instances at a hard
ceiling.

A single alert on the whole organization's spend is nearly useless -- by the time it fires,
nobody can tell what caused it. Budgets scoped per account, per service or per tag are what make
the alert actionable.

**Cost Anomaly Detection** is the complement and is often better than a threshold: it learns
each service's normal pattern and flags departures, so a new $400-a-day line item in a $50,000
account is caught even though no budget was crossed.
