Service Catalog lets a central team publish a list of approved, parameterised CloudFormation
templates -- **products** -- that other teams may launch, with the permissions attached to the
product rather than to the person.

That last clause is the whole mechanism. A **launch constraint** says "run this product using
this IAM role", so a developer with almost no permissions can provision a compliant RDS instance
with encryption, backups, Multi-AZ and the right subnet group -- because the role does it, not
them. They cannot create a database any other way, and they cannot use the role for anything
else.

Around that: **portfolios** group products and are shared with accounts or OUs; **TagOptions**
force the tags your cost allocation depends on; **template constraints** limit parameter values,
so "instance type" is a dropdown of three rather than a free-text field; and **provisioned
products** are tracked, so the catalogue knows what exists and can update it to a new version.

Where it shows up: Control Tower's **Account Factory** is a Service Catalog product, which is
the clearest example of the pattern. Beyond that it is the standard answer to "developers need
to provision infrastructure, but it must be compliant, and they cannot have the permissions to
build it by hand" -- a Professional-level question that appears regularly.

Its competition today is a well-run internal platform with Terraform modules or CDK constructs
in a pipeline, which gives the same guardrails with a developer experience teams tend to prefer.
Service Catalog's advantage is that it works through the console for people who do not write
infrastructure code, and that the permission model is enforced by AWS rather than by a pipeline.
