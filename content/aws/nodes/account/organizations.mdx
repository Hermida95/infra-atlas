An AWS account is the only hard boundary the platform has. Not a VPC, not a tag, not an IAM
policy -- an account. It is the boundary for blast radius, for service quotas, for billing, for
most compliance scoping, and for the default denial of everything outside it. Organizations is
what makes running many of them practical.

The structure is a **management account** at the root, **organizational units** forming a tree,
and member accounts in them. Policies attach to OUs and are inherited, so the OU design is the
policy design. A common shape: Security (log archive, audit), Infrastructure (networking, shared
services), Workloads (Prod, Non-Prod), Sandbox, and Suspended for accounts on their way out.

The management account should be empty. It is exempt from SCPs, it owns the billing and the
organization itself, and a compromise of it is a compromise of everything. Run nothing in it,
restrict it to a tiny number of people, and delegate administration of GuardDuty, Security Hub,
Config, IAM Access Analyzer, Backup and the rest to purpose-built accounts -- all of those
support a **delegated administrator** precisely so the management account does not become an
operations account by accident.

What the organization gives you beyond structure: **consolidated billing** with volume tiers and
Savings Plans pooled across every account; **service control policies** as guardrails;
**resource sharing** through RAM so one VPC or Transit Gateway serves many accounts;
**organization-wide** CloudTrail, Config, GuardDuty, Security Hub and Backup policies that cover
accounts created tomorrow; and **StackSets** for deploying a baseline everywhere.

The counter-argument people raise is that accounts add overhead. They do -- which is what
Control Tower, Account Factory and StackSets exist to remove. The overhead of *not* separating
is a single quota pool, a single bill nobody can attribute, and one mistake that reaches
everything.
