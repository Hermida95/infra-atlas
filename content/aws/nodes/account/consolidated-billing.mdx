Consolidated billing is the default in an organization: one invoice for every account, paid by
the management account. Beyond the convenience there are two effects that are worth real money.

**Volume tiering.** Usage is aggregated across accounts before tiered pricing is applied, so the
whole organization's S3 storage or data transfer reaches the cheaper tiers together rather than
each account starting at the top of the price list.

**Discount sharing.** Savings Plans and Reserved Instances bought in one account apply to
matching usage **anywhere in the organization**. This is why commitments are bought centrally by
whoever owns the bill, rather than by each team: a Compute Savings Plan purchased once covers
EC2, Fargate and Lambda in every account. Sharing can be turned off per account when a business
unit must not benefit from another's commitment.

The reporting side is what makes it usable. **Cost allocation tags** have to be activated in the
management account before they appear in any report, and only from that day forward -- the most
common reason a tagging strategy produces no data for months. The **Cost and Usage Report** is
the detailed, line-item-level export to S3 that feeds Athena or a third-party tool, and it is
the only source precise enough for real chargeback. **Cost Categories** group accounts and tags
into business units so the bill can be read by someone who does not know the account ids.

For separation, **billing conductor** and per-account budgets handle the "each team sees its own
spend" requirement without splitting the organization, which would lose both of the benefits
above.
