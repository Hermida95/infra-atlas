Choosing an instance starts with the letter, and the letter is the workload's bottleneck. A
memory-bound database on a `c` instance just costs more to be slow. The quick map: **t**
burstable for idle-most-of-the-time workloads, **m** balanced, **c** compute-optimised, **r**
memory-optimised (and **x**/**z** for extreme memory), **i** and **d** for local NVMe
throughput, **g**/**p**/**inf**/**trn** for GPUs and ML accelerators, **hpc** for tightly
coupled clusters.

**Graviton** -- the `g` suffix, as in `m7g` -- is AWS's own Arm processor, typically around 20%
cheaper than the x86 equivalent and frequently faster per vCPU. For anything running on an
interpreted or JIT runtime, or in a container that can be built multi-arch, it is close to free
money. The reason not to is a dependency with no Arm build.

**T instances** have a model of their own and it catches people out. They earn CPU credits at a
fixed rate and spend them when busy above a baseline. Run out and the instance throttles hard.
`unlimited` mode (the default on t3 and later) keeps the performance and charges for the surplus
instead -- fine for spiky workloads, expensive for a steadily busy one, where an `m` instance is
cheaper. A t-family instance behind a load balancer under sustained load is a classic wrong
answer.

Two details that decide designs. **Network and EBS bandwidth scale with size**, so a small
instance can be the bottleneck for a database long before its CPU is; `n` variants and
EBS-optimised bandwidth figures are in the documentation table, and that table is the right
place to settle an argument. And **`.metal`** gives you the bare host, which matters for
software licensed per socket and for hypervisors you bring yourself.

On sizing in general: measure before choosing, and prefer scaling out to scaling up when the
workload allows, because horizontal scale is also fault tolerance. Compute Optimizer reads your
real utilisation and recommends a type, which beats a guess and is free.
