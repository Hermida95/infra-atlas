The same instance has three prices, and picking the mix is an architecture decision. The shape
most mature accounts land on: a **committed baseline** covering the capacity that is always
running, **Spot** for the elastic and interruptible portion, and **on-demand** for what can be
neither.

**Spot** is spare capacity at up to 90% off. The price moves with supply and demand per instance
type per zone, you do not bid, and AWS reclaims the instance with a **two-minute warning**
delivered through instance metadata and an EventBridge event. The rules for using it well are
few and they all point the same way: be **flexible** -- accept many instance types and sizes
across several zones, because interruption risk is per pool and a request pinned to one type is
a request that gets interrupted; **handle the notice** by draining, checkpointing and
deregistering; and **keep state off the instance**. Use the `capacity-optimized` allocation
strategy, which picks the deepest pools rather than the cheapest, because an interruption costs
more than the few percent saved.

What suits it: CI runners, batch and data processing, rendering, stateless web tiers behind a
load balancer with an on-demand floor, EMR task nodes, ECS and EKS worker nodes. What does not:
a database, anything with a long non-resumable job, or a licence that charges per launch. **AWS
Batch** and **Fargate Spot** exist to make this easy, because in Batch every job is retryable by
definition.

**Savings Plans** are the commitment side, and the flexible kind is almost always right. A
*Compute* Savings Plan commits to an hourly spend and applies to EC2 in any family, size or
region, plus Fargate and Lambda -- so a migration to containers does not strand it. An *EC2
Instance* Savings Plan is cheaper and locks the family and region. Classic Reserved Instances
are mostly a legacy of the same idea, though they remain the way to reserve capacity in a
specific zone. One year of no-upfront is the low-risk starting point; three years all-upfront is
the maximum discount and the maximum bet.

**Capacity Reservations** are the piece people forget: they are not a discount, they guarantee
that capacity exists. For a disaster-recovery region where a failover has to be able to launch
two hundred instances at once, a reservation is the only thing that makes that a fact rather
than a hope.
