Nobody moves objects between classes by hand. A lifecycle configuration is a set of rules on the
bucket that transition and expire objects by age, and it is how archival actually happens.

A rule has a **filter** -- a prefix, a tag, an object size range, or nothing for the whole
bucket -- and then actions. **Transitions** move an object to another storage class after N
days. **Expiration** deletes it. There are separate actions for **noncurrent versions** in a
versioned bucket, which is where most forgotten spend lives, and for **incomplete multipart
uploads**, which is the one-line rule everyone should have: a failed large upload leaves parts
that are stored and billed and that no console listing shows.

A typical policy: Standard for 30 days, Standard-IA to 90, Glacier Flexible to a year, Deep
Archive after that, expire at seven years. Noncurrent versions to IA after 30 days and deleted
after 90. Incomplete multipart uploads aborted after 7 days.

Three things to know before relying on it. Transitions **cost money per thousand objects**, so
moving millions of tiny objects can cost more than the storage it saves -- another argument for
Intelligent-Tiering on small objects. Transitions only go **down** the cost ladder; bringing an
object back means a restore or a copy. And the minimum duration charges still apply: an object
transitioned to Glacier and deleted a week later is billed for 90 days.

Lifecycle is also the enforcement half of a data retention policy. **Object Lock** with a
retention period is the other half, and the two together are what stops a backup being deleted
by an attacker or by a lifecycle rule nobody reviewed.
