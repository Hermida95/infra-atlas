An EBS volume is block storage attached over the network to one instance, in one Availability
Zone. It survives the instance stopping and being terminated (if you clear "delete on
termination"), and it does not survive the zone. Everything about EBS design follows from those
two facts.

**gp3** is the right default and the reason is pricing: it includes 3,000 IOPS and 125 MB/s at
any size, and lets you provision more of either independently. gp2 tied IOPS to size at 3 per
GB, which meant over-provisioning a terabyte to get IOPS you needed from a 100 GB volume. If you
have gp2 volumes, moving them to gp3 is usually a straight saving with no downtime.

**io2 Block Express** is for when you can measure the need: provisioned IOPS up to 256,000,
consistent sub-millisecond latency, and 99.999% durability rather than 99.8-99.9%. It is also
the only type that supports **Multi-Attach** -- the same volume on up to 16 instances -- and
that comes with a warning: there is no coordination, so it requires a cluster-aware filesystem.
Attaching ext4 or NTFS to two writers corrupts it.

**st1** and **sc1** are spinning disks. They are cheap and they are good at exactly one thing:
large sequential reads. Random I/O on them is miserable. They cannot be boot volumes.

Two operational points. Volumes can be **modified live** -- size, type, IOPS -- without
detaching, though the filesystem still needs growing afterwards and there is a cooldown before
the next change. And **snapshots** are incremental against the previous snapshot but each one is
independently restorable, so deleting an old snapshot never breaks a newer one. Snapshots are
how a volume crosses a zone or a region, and **Data Lifecycle Manager** or **AWS Backup** is
what should be taking them on a schedule rather than a cron job on the instance.

Performance has a ceiling you do not control: the instance. Network and EBS bandwidth scale with
instance size, so a small instance will cap a fast volume long before the volume does.
