A role is an identity nobody owns. It has permissions but no password and no access key, and the
only way to use it is to ask STS for a temporary session, which expires. That single property is
why "use a role" is the right answer to almost every question about credentials: there is
nothing to leak that is still valid tomorrow.

Every role carries two policies and they answer different questions. The **trust policy** says
*who may assume this role*, and it is a resource policy: the role is the resource. The
**permissions policy** says *what the session may then do*. Breaking into a role means getting
past the trust policy, so that is the one to read first and the one to write carefully. A trust
policy naming `"Service": "ec2.amazonaws.com"` lets any EC2 instance in the account that gets
this instance profile assume it; one naming `"AWS": "arn:aws:iam::111122223333:root"` lets
*anyone in that whole account who also has `sts:AssumeRole` in their own policy* assume it,
which is why cross-account trust is usually narrowed to a specific role ARN.

How the role reaches the workload differs by service and the distinction matters. On EC2 it is
an **instance profile**, and the credentials appear at the instance metadata endpoint -- which
is why IMDSv2 is not optional: IMDSv1 lets a server-side request forgery bug in your application
read the role's credentials with a single GET. On Lambda it is the **execution role**, the
single security boundary of the function. On ECS there are two, and conflating them costs an
afternoon: the **task execution role** is what pulls the image and writes the log stream, the
**task role** is what your code uses. On EKS, **IRSA** or **Pod Identity** maps a Kubernetes
service account to a role, so a pod gets its own identity rather than the node's.

For anything outside AWS -- a CI pipeline, a partner's SaaS -- the pattern is federation, not a
user with keys. A GitHub Actions workflow presents an OIDC token and the trust policy pins the
repository and even the branch, so a fork cannot deploy. For a third-party vendor, require an
`sts:ExternalId` they alone know: without it, a vendor who manages many customers can be talked
into using *your* role on another customer's behalf -- the confused-deputy problem, and the
reason the field exists.
