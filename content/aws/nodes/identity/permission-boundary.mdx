A boundary is a ceiling, not a grant. You attach a policy to a user or a role as its permission
boundary, and from then on that principal's effective permissions are the intersection of its
own policies and the boundary. Giving it `AdministratorAccess` changes nothing if the boundary
only allows S3 and CloudWatch.

The reason it exists is delegation. In a large organization, platform teams cannot be the
bottleneck for every IAM change, but letting a developer create roles is letting them create a
role with `*:*` and assume it. The pattern that solves it: grant `iam:CreateRole` and
`iam:AttachRolePolicy` on the condition `iam:PermissionsBoundary` equals your boundary policy's
ARN, and deny `iam:DeleteRolePermissionsBoundary`. Now the developer can create and manage roles
all day, and every role they create is born under the same ceiling they are.

The distinction from an SCP is the scope. An SCP caps an entire account and is attached in
Organizations; a boundary caps one principal and is attached in IAM. They stack: a request has
to be allowed by the identity policy, the boundary, the SCP and, for cross-account, the resource
policy. Boundaries do not apply to service-linked roles, and they never grant anything on their
own -- a principal with a boundary and no identity policy can do nothing at all.
