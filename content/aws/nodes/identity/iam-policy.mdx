A policy is a JSON document, and the only thing that makes it hard is that four different
documents can apply to one request at once. The evaluation order is fixed, and it never changes:
an explicit `Deny` in any of them ends the request immediately. If nothing denies, the request
needs an `Allow` from the identity's own policy -- or, for a cross-account call, from the
resource's policy -- and it must also survive every ceiling above it: the service control policy
of the account, a permission boundary on the principal, and any session policy passed when the
role was assumed. The effective permission is the intersection of all of them, never the union.
There is no "grant" that can lift a ceiling.

The parts of a statement that people write are `Effect`, `Action` and `Resource`. The part that
makes a policy good is `Condition`. A policy that says "this role may read this bucket" is a
blunt instrument; one that adds `aws:SourceVpce` turns it into "this role may read this bucket
only through our VPC endpoint", which is the difference between a credential leak being an
incident and being a non-event. Other conditions worth knowing by name: `aws:SecureTransport` to
require HTTPS, `aws:MultiFactorAuthPresent` for anything destructive, `aws:PrincipalOrgID` to
allow the whole organization without listing account ids, and `aws:PrincipalTag` matched against
`aws:ResourceTag` for attribute-based access control, which is how one policy serves twenty
teams instead of twenty policies serving one each.

Two traps. The first is wildcards in `Action` with a narrow-looking `Resource`: `"Action":
"s3:*"` on one bucket still includes `s3:DeleteBucket` and `s3:PutBucketPolicy`, so the holder
can grant themselves more. The second is `NotAction` with `Allow`, which means "everything
except these" -- including every service AWS launches next quarter.

Least privilege is reached by subtraction, not by guessing. IAM Access Analyzer reads the last
ninety days of CloudTrail for a role and generates the policy that covers only what was actually
called; the policy simulator then answers whether a specific request would be allowed, and which
statement decided it. Both beat reasoning about the JSON in your head.
