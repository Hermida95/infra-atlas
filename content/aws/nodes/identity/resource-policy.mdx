Most of IAM is attached to the caller. A resource-based policy is attached to the thing being
called, and it answers a question an identity policy cannot: *who, in any account, may touch
this?* S3 bucket policies, KMS key policies, SQS and SNS access policies, Lambda resource
policies, ECR repository policies, Secrets Manager resource policies and API Gateway resource
policies are all the same mechanism.

The field that only exists here is `Principal`, and it is what makes cross-account access
possible without creating anything in the other account. For most services, cross-account access
needs **both** ends to agree: an identity policy in the calling account allowing the action, and
a resource policy in the owning account allowing that principal. KMS is the famous exception in
the other direction -- the key policy must allow it, and an IAM policy alone is never enough,
which is why a key with a careless policy can lock out the account's own administrator with no
way back.

Two patterns worth having by heart. The **anti-exfiltration pair**: a bucket policy with
`"Condition": {"StringNotEquals": {"aws:SourceVpce": "vpce-..."}}` and `"Effect": "Deny"`, plus
an endpoint policy listing the buckets that endpoint may reach. Credentials stolen from that
environment are then useless anywhere else, and data cannot be copied to a bucket outside the
list. The **organization-wide grant**: `"Condition": {"StringEquals": {"aws:PrincipalOrgID":
"o-abc123"}}`, which allows every account in the organization including the ones created
tomorrow, with no list of account ids to maintain.

A bucket policy is also where you enforce the blanket rules: deny anything where
`aws:SecureTransport` is false, and deny `s3:PutObject` without the encryption header you
expect. Those are statements nobody can forget to apply, because they live on the bucket.
