An Availability Zone is one or more discrete data centres with independent power, cooling and
networking, inside a region. The design of the platform rests on two facts about them: they are
far enough apart to fail independently, and close enough -- single-digit milliseconds -- to
replicate synchronously.

That combination is why high availability on AWS is a configuration rather than a project.
Synchronous replication across zones is what makes RDS Multi-AZ possible with no data loss;
independent failure is what makes it worth having.

**Zone names are per account.** `eu-west-1a` in your account and in mine are probably different
physical zones -- AWS randomises the mapping so that everyone does not crowd into "a". When zone
identity actually matters across accounts, such as placing resources in the same physical zone
to avoid cross-zone data charges in a shared VPC, use the **Availability Zone ID** (`euw1-az1`),
which is consistent everywhere.

**Zonal versus regional** is the distinction to carry into every design. A subnet, an EC2
instance, an EBS volume and a NAT Gateway are zonal: they exist in one zone and die with it. A
load balancer, an Auto Scaling group, S3, DynamoDB, SQS and Lambda are regional: they already
span zones. The work in a highly available design is entirely in the first list.

**Cross-zone data transfer is charged** in both directions, which is a real line on a chatty
workload's bill and the reason a single NAT Gateway serving three zones costs more than three
serving one each. It is also why some latency-critical designs deliberately keep a request
inside one zone and accept the availability trade -- a decision worth making explicitly rather
than discovering in the bill.
