A region is a named geographic area -- `eu-west-1`, `us-east-1` -- containing three or more
Availability Zones. It is the first decision in any design, and it is made on four grounds.

**Where the users are**, because distance is round trips and no amount of compute fixes the
speed of light. **Where the data is allowed to be**, which for personal data in Europe or health
data under national rules is a hard constraint rather than a preference. **What is available**,
because not every service launches in every region and some never arrive. And **price**, which
differs materially: the same instance can cost 20-40% more in some regions than in `us-east-1`.

The distinction that organises everything else is **scope**. Some services are **global** --
IAM, Route 53, CloudFront, WAF for CloudFront, Organizations -- and their console always talks
to `us-east-1`, which is why a certificate for CloudFront must be issued there. Most services
are **regional**: S3, DynamoDB, SQS, Lambda already span the zones and you never see the
machines. A few are **zonal**: EC2 instances, EBS volumes and subnets live in one zone, and
these are the ones you have to place and replicate yourself.

**Nothing crosses a region boundary by accident.** No replication, no failover, no backup copy
happens unless you configure it -- and that is a safety property as much as a limitation. It is
also why a multi-region design is a separate project rather than a setting, and why quotas,
AMIs, KMS keys, certificates and secrets all have to be created again on the other side.

Two region-specific details worth carrying. `us-east-1` hosts several global control planes, so
it is both the cheapest region and the one whose incidents have the widest reach. And some
regions are **opt-in** and disabled by default, which is a useful thing to leave that way and to
enforce with an SCP on `aws:RequestedRegion`.
