Edge locations are AWS points of presence -- several hundred of them, in far more cities than
there are regions. They are not regions: you cannot run an instance there. What they do is
terminate connections close to the user.

That matters more than bandwidth. A TLS handshake is several round trips, so a user in Singapore
reaching a region in Ireland pays that distance repeatedly before any content moves. Terminating
at a Singapore edge and crossing the rest once, on a warm connection over the AWS backbone,
removes most of the latency even when nothing is cached.

Four services run there. **CloudFront** caches and serves HTTP, and runs **CloudFront
Functions** and **Lambda@Edge**. **Global Accelerator** takes TCP and UDP connections at the
edge and routes them over the backbone. **Route 53** answers DNS from the edge, which is why
DNS-based failover reacts in seconds anywhere in the world. And **Shield** absorbs volumetric
attacks there, before they reach a region.

Behind the edge locations sit **regional edge caches**, a mid-tier layer that holds less popular
content longer, so a cache miss at the edge often does not reach your origin either.

The practical consequence for a design: putting CloudFront in front of an application is usually
the cheapest latency improvement available, and often reduces cost at the same time, because
traffic from AWS origins to CloudFront is not charged for data transfer.
