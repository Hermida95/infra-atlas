Outposts are racks of AWS-designed hardware installed in your own data centre, running the same
APIs, the same services and the same tooling as a region. AWS owns, monitors, patches and
replaces the hardware; you provide space, power, cooling and network.

Two forms: full **42U racks** for substantial capacity, and **1U/2U servers** for small sites,
retail branches and factory floors. They run EC2, EBS, S3 on Outposts, ECS, EKS, RDS and
ElastiCache -- a subset, chosen for the workloads people actually need on premises.

Three requirements lead here, and they are the ones an exam will describe rather than name.
**Latency** to equipment or users in the building, measured in single-digit milliseconds, where
even a Local Zone is too far. **Data residency**, where regulation or contract says the data
must physically remain on your premises. And **local processing of large data** -- a factory or
a research facility generating more than the link to a region can carry.

The dependency to understand is the **service link**: a connection back to the parent region
that carries the control plane. The Outpost keeps running local workloads if it drops, but you
cannot launch, modify or manage anything until it returns, so the link is usually a Direct
Connect, often redundant. Capacity is also fixed -- you ordered a certain number of instance
slots, and there is no elastic scaling beyond them.

Against the neighbours: **Local Zones** are AWS facilities in a city with nothing to install;
**Outposts** is hardware on your floor; **Snowball Edge Compute** is the ruggedised, temporary,
disconnected option for a ship or a remote site.
