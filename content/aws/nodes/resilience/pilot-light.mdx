The data is already in the second region and continuously replicating; the compute is configured
but switched off or scaled to nothing. On failover you start it, point traffic at it, and the
data is already there.

**RPO** is seconds to minutes, because replication is continuous -- a cross-region read replica,
an Aurora global secondary, DynamoDB global tables, S3 replication. **RTO** is tens of minutes,
because everything that serves traffic has to start: instances launch, containers pull images,
caches are cold, connection pools fill, and the database has to be promoted.

What stays on is deliberately minimal: the database replica, the replicated object storage, and
the infrastructure definitions. Auto Scaling groups sit at zero, load balancers may exist but
have no targets, and the container images and AMIs are replicated so there is nothing to build.

The two things that decide whether it meets its RTO are both measurable, and both are usually
optimistic in the plan. **Cold start time** for the whole stack, including anything that warms
up slowly, and **capacity availability** in the second region at the moment you need it -- which
is what Capacity Reservations exist for when the failover must be certain.

It sits between backup-and-restore (cheaper, hours) and warm standby (more expensive, minutes).
The step up to warm standby is not conceptual: it is running a small amount of each tier instead
of none, which removes the cold-start risk and lets you actually serve while scaling. If the RTO
is near the edge of what pilot light can do, warm standby is usually the honest choice.
