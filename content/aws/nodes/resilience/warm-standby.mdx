A complete, working copy of the stack in the second region, scaled down, taking no production
traffic -- or a trickle of it. On failover you scale it up and shift the traffic.

**RPO** is seconds, from continuous replication. **RTO** is minutes, and crucially the *service*
is already running: it can start serving the moment traffic arrives, degraded, while the
scale-up happens behind it. That is the real difference from pilot light, and the reason it is
the usual answer for a serious RTO.

It is also the strategy that is actually testable. Because the second region is running, you can
send it real traffic -- a small weighted share through Route 53, or a synthetic canary --
continuously. A standby that serves 1% of requests every day is a standby you know works. One
that has never served a request is a configuration you hope works, and those usually have one
thing missing: a secret not replicated, a quota too low, a security group referencing an ID that
does not exist in that region.

The cost is a fraction of production -- minimum-size instances, fewer of them, smaller database
readers -- plus the data replication, which at volume can be the larger line. Budget for the
scale-up too: the plan must state what size it goes to and the account must have the quota to
get there.

The components: Aurora Global Database or DynamoDB global tables for the data, S3 replication
for objects, infrastructure deployed by the same templates in both regions, Route 53 health
checks with failover records or Global Accelerator for the switch, and a rehearsal schedule.
Fault Injection Service is the thing that turns the rehearsal from an intention into a recurring
event.
