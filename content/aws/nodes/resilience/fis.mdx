Fault Injection Service breaks things on purpose, in a controlled way, so that the failure modes
you designed for are ones you have watched happen.

The actions are real faults, not simulations: stop or terminate instances, fail over an RDS
cluster, throttle API calls to a service, inject latency or packet loss on an instance, exhaust
CPU or memory, interrupt a Spot instance, disrupt connectivity to a subnet or an Availability
Zone, kill ECS tasks or EKS pods.

The safety mechanism is what makes it usable in production. An experiment template has
**targets** selected by tag or resource id, **stop conditions** bound to CloudWatch alarms, and
an IAM role scoping what it may touch. If an alarm trips -- error rate, latency, a business
metric -- the experiment halts and rolls back automatically. That is what turns a game day into
something that can be scheduled rather than something that requires a meeting.

The questions worth running an experiment against are the ones every design claims to have
answered. Does the Auto Scaling group actually replace a terminated instance fast enough? Does
the RDS failover complete inside the window, and does the connection pool recover or sit on a
cached DNS entry? Does losing a zone cost a third of capacity or all of it? Does the Spot
interruption handler drain properly? Does the cross-region failover runbook work when the person
who wrote it is on holiday?

Run them on a schedule, in production or in an environment that is genuinely like it, and record
the measured RTO -- because that number, not the one in the plan, is the real one.
