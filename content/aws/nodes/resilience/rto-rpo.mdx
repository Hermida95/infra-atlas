**RPO** is how much data you can afford to lose: the gap between the last recoverable state and
the failure. Daily snapshots mean an RPO of up to 24 hours. Continuous replication means
seconds. **RTO** is how long you can afford to be down: the time from failure to service
restored.

These two numbers decide the strategy, and nothing else in the scenario does. Hours of both
means backup and restore. Tens of minutes means pilot light. Minutes means warm standby.
Near-zero means active/active. An exam question that states the numbers has already told you the
answer; the rest of the scenario is there to make you check which services can meet them.

The discipline worth practising is deriving them per workload rather than per company. Asking a
business owner "how much downtime is acceptable" always returns zero. Asking "what does an hour
of this being down cost, and what would you spend to avoid it" returns a real answer, and it is
different for the checkout path and for the internal reporting tool. Running the whole estate at
the strictest number is how disaster recovery budgets get rejected.

Two things people forget to count in RTO. **Detection and decision** time: if nobody notices for
twenty minutes and the decision to fail over takes ten more, a five-minute technical recovery is
a thirty-five-minute outage. And **dependencies**: the application's RTO cannot be better than
the RTO of the database, DNS, identity provider and third-party APIs it needs.

Finally, both numbers are claims until they are tested. A rehearsal -- a game day, a Fault
Injection Service experiment, a real restore -- turns them into measurements. What the last
rehearsal took is the RTO; everything else is a plan.
