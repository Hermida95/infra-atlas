Beyond zones and regions there is a third axis of failure containment: dividing the workload
itself so that one bad input, one noisy tenant or one bad deployment cannot reach everyone.

A **cell** is a complete, independent copy of the stack -- its own compute, its own data, its
own limits -- serving a subset of customers. Cells share nothing, so a failure inside one is
bounded by definition. Deployments go cell by cell, which also makes a bad release a partial
outage rather than a total one. The routing layer in front is deliberately as simple as
possible, because it is the only shared component and therefore the only one whose failure is
total.

**Shuffle sharding** is the refinement that makes it powerful at small cell counts. Instead of
assigning each customer to one cell, assign each to a random *combination* of two cells from the
pool. With eight cells there are 28 pairs, so two customers sharing an exact pair is rare; one
customer causing a problem affects their two cells, and almost no other customer loses both. The
blast radius drops dramatically without multiplying the infrastructure.

On AWS the building blocks are familiar: a queue and a concurrency limit per tenant so one
backlog cannot starve the others, **reserved concurrency** on Lambda per cell, separate DynamoDB
tables or partitions, separate Auto Scaling groups, **partition placement groups** for
rack-level isolation, and **bulkheads** -- fixed resource pools per dependency so one slow
downstream cannot consume every thread in the application.

It is a Professional-level topic because the trade is real: more cells means more operational
surface, more deployment time and more cost. The question it answers is "when this fails, how
many customers notice", and for some systems that is the only availability number that matters.
