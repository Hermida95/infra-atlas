Multi-AZ is the baseline, and it is cheap enough that there is rarely an argument. Availability
Zones are physically separate data centres with independent power, cooling and networking, far
enough apart to fail independently and close enough -- single-digit milliseconds -- to replicate
synchronously. That combination is what makes high availability on AWS a configuration rather
than a project.

The checklist, by layer. **Subnets** in at least two zones, symmetric in size. **Load
balancers** across those subnets, with cross-zone balancing so one zone's loss becomes a
capacity question rather than an outage. **Auto Scaling groups** spanning the same zones, with
enough headroom that losing a third of capacity does not take the rest down. **Databases** with
Multi-AZ enabled -- RDS with a synchronous standby, Aurora with storage already across three
zones. **NAT Gateways** one per zone, with each private subnet routing to its own, or a zone
failure takes out the others' egress. **EFS** with mount targets in every zone, or S3 and
DynamoDB which are regional and already span zones for you.

Two details that undo the rest. **Instance-store data and EBS volumes are zonal**, so anything
stateful on them is a single-zone dependency however many zones the fleet spans. And capacity:
after losing a zone, the survivors must be able to absorb the load -- if the group is at 90%
utilisation across three zones, two zones cannot carry it, and the Auto Scaling group may not be
able to launch replacements fast enough or at all if the region is under pressure.

What Multi-AZ does **not** protect against: a region-wide service problem, a bad deployment, a
deleted database, a corrupted record or a compromised account. Those are what cross-region
strategies, backups and immutable vaults are for, and conflating the two is the most common gap
in a design that calls itself highly available.
